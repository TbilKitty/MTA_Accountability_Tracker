What MTA and City Schools could measure, disclose, and change

Maryland requires fare-free transit for eligible Baltimore City students, but no one is required to report whether that service gets them to school on time. This page asks four questions about closing that gap.

How to read the labels on this page

Question 1

What is required today?

The student transit statute: fare-free rides, no arrival standard

Maryland Transportation § 7–710 requires MTA to provide rides on its transit vehicles to eligible Baltimore City public school students (§ 7–710(a)(1)). The service covers school-related and educational extracurricular activities, on and off campus, between 5 a.m. and 8 p.m. (§ 7–710(b)(1)). MTA may not collect fees or reimbursement for these rides (§ 7–710(c)), which is what makes them fare-free. MTA, City Schools and the Mayor’s Office of Employment Development set student eligibility by regulation (§ 7–710(d); COMAR 11.06.05). The same section also covers Baltimore City YouthWorks summer job participants.

The statute sets no arrival-before-the-bell standard. It does not set a punctuality target for the buses students ride, and it does not require MTA or City Schools to report how well that service gets students to school. Its last subsection is (d).

MTA’s own punctuality measure, a separate thing

Agency benchmark MTA counts a local bus as on time when it reaches a scheduled timepoint from two minutes early to seven minutes late, and it sets an 80% on-time goal for core bus service. This is an agency performance measure, not a requirement of § 7–710. It describes buses at timepoints across the whole network, not students getting to school. The tracker uses the same two-early, seven-late window.

Question 2

What does our evidence show?

Tracker result

On the same routes and school dates, morning MTA bus stop events in Baltimore City’s highest-poverty census tracts were canceled or more than seven minutes late 3.1 percentage points more often than in the lowest-poverty tracts (October 30 – November 20, 2026).

This compares stops on the same bus route and school date, within the same morning commute. A gap of 3.1 percentage points means about three more disruptions (cancellations or delays over seven minutes) per 100 classified stop events. Across all classified events, without matching routes and dates, the morning rates were 34.0% versus 28.0%.

The tracker observes bus stop events: whether a scheduled bus reached a timepoint stop, how early or late, and whether MTA’s feed marked the trip canceled. It cannot see riders. It cannot establish that any student was late, that instruction was missed, or that the law was violated, and § 7–710 sets no punctuality standard to violate.

Question 3

What records would help?

These are records worth requesting from MTA and Baltimore City Public Schools. Some may not exist in this form, and some may be withheld or released only in aggregate, so they are listed as records to seek, not records that are known to exist.

Record to seekFromThe gap it would fill
The current student transportation agreement, with any amendmentsMTA and City SchoolsShows what each agency has agreed to provide and pay for beyond the statute’s fare-free requirement, and whether the agreement says anything about service on school-serving routes.
Any service or reporting commitments made under that agreement or elsewhereMTA and City SchoolsShows whether a punctuality, service-level or reporting promise already exists, who made it, and who is supposed to receive the reports.
Scheduled versus operated trips on school-serving routes, by date and commuteMTASeparates trips that never ran from buses that ran late. The tracker infers this from a public feed in which a missed trip and a bus with failed tracking equipment look the same.
Cancellation reasons for those tripsMTAShows why trips were canceled (for example, staffing, vehicle breakdowns or detours), which points to who could fix the problem. The public feed gives no reason.
Any existing aggregate data on transportation-related tardinessCity SchoolsLinks bus disruptions to the outcome that matters, students reaching class before the bell, which the tracker cannot observe.

Question 4

What could Baltimore do with those records?

MTA and City Schools could publish a joint school-commute scorecard for the routes, trips and stops students use, separately for the morning and afternoon:

  • Agency benchmarkTrips that ran: the share of scheduled trips that operated, with canceled trips reported separately from punctuality.
  • Agency benchmarkPunctuality at key stops: the share of buses that served key school-route stops within MTA’s on-time window (the tracker already estimates this from the public feed).
  • Proposed student outcomeArrival before the first bell: the share of observed student journeys that reached school before its scheduled start, if that outcome can be measured. The tracker cannot measure it.
  • Follow-up on recurring problems: for routes with recurring disruptions, who is responsible for the corrective action and when performance will be reviewed again.

WMATA is a model for reporting missed trips separately from punctuality. New York City’s § 21-994 is a model for recording whether a delay prevented a student from arriving at school.

What other systems measure

WMATA is the public-transit comparison for Maryland MTA. New York City and Boston show how school systems report on dedicated student transportation.

WMATA Metrobus (public transit)

Agency benchmark

WMATA reports bus punctuality and missed trips as separate measures. On-time performance is the share of timepoints a bus reached from 2 minutes early to 7 minutes late, counting only trips that ran. Service delivered is the share of scheduled trips that actually ran. WMATA FY25 measure definitions

75.7% on time and 97.4% of scheduled service delivered: published FY 2025 systemwide results (July 2024 – June 2025). WMATA FY 2025 Service Excellence Report

Maryland MTA bus (public transit)

Agency benchmark

MTA reports on-time performance against an 80% goal for core bus service. MTA performance measures

73% of bus service on time: published FY 2025 systemwide result. Maryland budget analysis, FY 2027, p. 9

MTA’s own page defines on time as two minutes early to seven minutes late; the budget analysis that reports the 73% describes it as generally within 10 minutes of schedule.

N.Y.C. Admin. Code § 21-994 (school buses)

Agency benchmark

A reporting requirement for school-bus delays. The Department of Education must report each delay that bus vendors report, with its length, cause, vendor and school, and “whether or not the delay resulted in the failure to arrive at school.”

N.Y.C. Admin. Code § 21-994 · readable copy (Local Law 34 of 2019, intro.nyc)

Boston Public Schools school-bus on-time performance

Agency benchmark

Shows how a school district reports the reliability of dedicated student transportation: the share of school bus routes that reach school before the bell in the morning, and before the scheduled departure time in the afternoon.

2025–26 school year through April: 90.7% of morning routes and 85.1% of afternoon routes on time. These are district school buses, not MBTA service. BPS on-time performance memo, May 2026

What Maryland could require

Proposed language · not current law

Proposed new subsection (e) of Maryland Transportation § 7–710:

(e)(1) The Administration, in coordination with Baltimore City Public Schools, shall publish a quarterly report on transit service used to transport eligible students to and from school. The Administration and the school system shall jointly identify the routes, trips, and stops covered by the report.

(2) The report shall include, separately for morning and afternoon school travel:

(i) The number of scheduled trips, the number that operated, and the number that were canceled or did not operate, with missing data reported separately.

(ii) The percentage of observed buses that served identified school-route stops within the published on-time window, with early departures and late arrivals reported separately.

(iii) The percentage of observed student journeys that reached school before the first bell, where reliable data are available, together with the number of journeys observed and any known gaps in coverage.

(3) The Administration and the school system shall jointly establish and publish performance targets for the measures in paragraph (2). If a target is missed, the next report shall identify the affected routes or schools, the corrective action, the entity responsible for carrying it out, and the date progress will be reviewed.

(4) Any student outcome data published under this subsection shall be aggregated and subject to safeguards that prevent identification of individual students.

In this proposal, (2)(i) and (2)(ii) would be Agency benchmark measures; (2)(iii) is a Proposed student outcome that no agency reports today and the tracker cannot measure.

The tracker can show where buses were late or canceled. This proposal would require MTA and City Schools to show whether the service ran as planned and whether students reached class before the bell.
