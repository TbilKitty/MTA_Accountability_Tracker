What MTA and City Schools could measure, disclose, and change

Maryland requires fare-free transit for eligible Baltimore City students, but no one is required to report whether that service gets them to school on time. This page asks five questions about closing that gap.

Question 1

What is required today?

The student transit statute: fare-free rides, no arrival standard

What the statute does: student fares. Maryland Transportation § 7–710 requires MTA to provide rides on its transit vehicles to eligible Baltimore City public school students (§ 7–710(a)(1)). The service covers school-related and educational extracurricular activities, on and off campus, between 5 a.m. and 8 p.m. (§ 7–710(b)(1)). MTA may not collect fees or reimbursement for these rides (§ 7–710(c)), which is what makes them fare-free. MTA, City Schools and the Mayor’s Office of Employment Development set student eligibility by regulation (§ 7–710(d); COMAR 11.06.05). The same section also covers Baltimore City YouthWorks summer job participants.

What the statute does not do: reliability. The statute sets no arrival-before-the-bell standard. It does not set a punctuality target for the buses students ride, and it does not require MTA or City Schools to report how well that service gets students to school. Its last subsection is (d).

MTA’s own punctuality measure, a separate thing

Agency benchmark MTA counts a local bus as on time when it reaches a scheduled timepoint from two minutes early to seven minutes late, and it sets an 80% on-time goal for core bus service. This is an agency performance measure, not a requirement of § 7–710. It describes buses at timepoints across the whole network, not students getting to school. The tracker uses the same two-early, seven-late window.

Any reliability or reporting standard, like the draft in Question 5, would be a new requirement. It would sit beside the fare provisions, not inside them: free fares say nothing about whether the bus comes.

Question 2

What does our evidence show?

Tracker result

Data coverage: October 30 – November 20, 2026

On the same routes and school dates, morning MTA bus stop events in Baltimore City’s highest-poverty census tracts were canceled or more than seven minutes late 3.1 percentage points more often than in the lowest-poverty tracts (October 30 – November 20, 2026).

This compares stops on the same bus route and school date, within the same morning commute. A gap of 3.1 percentage points means about three more disruptions (cancellations or delays over seven minutes) per 100 classified stop events. Across all classified events, without matching routes and dates, the morning rates were 34.0% versus 28.0%.

The tracker observes bus stop events: whether a scheduled bus reached a timepoint stop, how early or late, and whether MTA’s feed marked the trip canceled. It cannot see riders. It cannot establish that any student was late, that instruction was missed, or that the law was violated, and § 7–710 sets no punctuality standard to violate.

Question 3

What has MTA reported over time?

To compare our autumn 2026 sample with earlier years, and with Baltimore’s graduation and dropout trends, we looked for an official Baltimore bus cancellation series: canceled or missed trips as a share of scheduled trips. We checked MTA’s performance page, the MDOT Annual Attainment Report (2026), and the Department of Legislative Services’ budget analyses of MTA for fiscal 2025, 2026 and 2027.

None of them reports a separate cancellation or missed-trip rate for Baltimore local buses. They report on-time performance only.

That is the cancellation-data gap. Without a cancellation series, there is nothing comparable to set beside the district’s graduation and dropout series year by year, so this site does not draw that chart. On-time performance is a different measure: it describes buses that ran, so a canceled trip does not show up as late.

The report’s target for local bus is 85% by 2030; MTA’s own core bus goal is 80%. The legislative budget analyses for fiscal 2025, 2026 and 2027 give the same 73% for fiscal 2023, 2024 and 2025, and describe each mode’s on-time threshold as “generally within 10 minutes of the scheduled time.” MTA’s page notes that older bus figures used schedule and headway adherence, so early years may not be fully comparable.

One school-specific report, tied to one budget year

Agency benchmark The fiscal 2024 budget bill withheld MTA funds until MTA reported on its supplemental school trips (extra buses MTA runs for City Schools students under § 7–710) for the 2022–23 school year. MTA ran about 7,600 of these trips (136 a school day, serving 27 schools). Counting drop-offs from 2 minutes early to 7 minutes late as on time, 81% were on time or early, 14% were 7 to 15 minutes late, 4% were 15 to 30 minutes late and 1% were more than 30 minutes late. As the legislative analysts summarize it, the report covered late drop-offs, route diversions and the causes of delays; the summary gives no count of trips that never ran, and the report covered only the supplemental trips, not the regular routes students also ride. The requirement was attached to one budget year, and we found no recurring version of it.

Question 5

What could change?

MTA and City Schools could publish a joint school-commute scorecard for the routes, trips and stops students use, separately for the morning and afternoon:

  • Agency benchmarkTrips that ran: the share of scheduled trips that operated, with canceled trips reported separately from punctuality.
  • Agency benchmarkPunctuality at key stops: the share of buses that served key school-route stops within MTA’s on-time window (the tracker already estimates this from the public feed).
  • Proposed student outcomeArrival before the first bell: the share of observed student journeys that reached school before its scheduled start, if that outcome can be measured. The tracker cannot measure it.
  • Follow-up on recurring problems: for routes with recurring disruptions, who is responsible for the corrective action and when performance will be reviewed again.

WMATA is a model for reporting missed trips separately from punctuality. New York City’s § 21-994 is a model for recording whether a delay prevented a student from arriving at school.

What other systems measure

WMATA is the public-transit comparison for Maryland MTA. New York City and Boston show how school systems report on dedicated student transportation.

WMATA Metrobus (public transit)

Agency benchmark

WMATA reports bus punctuality and missed trips as separate measures. On-time performance is the share of timepoints a bus reached from 2 minutes early to 7 minutes late, counting only trips that ran. Service delivered is the share of scheduled trips that actually ran. WMATA Service Excellence Report measure definitions

75.7% on time and 97.4% of scheduled service delivered: published FY 2025 systemwide Metrobus results (July 2024 – June 2025). WMATA FY 2025 Service Excellence Report

These figures describe public bus service for all riders across the WMATA system. They do not measure whether students reached school on time.

Maryland MTA bus (public transit)

Agency benchmark

MTA reports on-time performance against an 80% goal for core bus service. MTA performance measures

MTA’s own page defines on time as two minutes early to seven minutes late; the budget analysis that reports the 73% describes it as generally within 10 minutes of schedule.

N.Y.C. Admin. Code § 21-994 (school buses)

Agency benchmark

A reporting requirement for school-bus delays. The Department of Education must report each delay that bus vendors report, with its length, cause, vendor and school, and “whether or not the delay resulted in the failure to arrive at school.”

The missing link is a data question

No public record today connects a canceled or late bus to a student’s attendance, tardiness or later school outcomes. That is not proof that no connection exists; it is a gap in what is collected and reported. MTA knows which trips ran; City Schools knows who was marked tardy or absent, and (through the fare program) which students hold transit passes. Neither is required to put the two together, even in aggregate. Whether commute disruptions affect attendance and outcomes is therefore first an accountability question: who should measure it, and who should have to report it.

What Maryland could require

Proposed language · not current law

Proposed new subsection (e) of Maryland Transportation § 7–710. Subsections (a) through (d), the fare-free ride provisions, would not change.

(e)(1) The Administration, in coordination with Baltimore City Public Schools, shall publish a quarterly report on transit service used to transport eligible students to and from school, including supplemental school service. The Administration and the school system shall jointly identify the routes, trips, and stops covered by the report.

(2) The report shall include, separately for morning and afternoon school travel and for each identified route:

(i) Cancellations: the number of scheduled trips, the number that operated, and the number that were canceled or did not operate, with trips for which no operating data exist reported separately.

(ii) Early departures: the number and percentage of observed bus departures from identified school-route stops more than 2 minutes before the scheduled time.

(iii) Delays: the number and percentage of observed bus arrivals at identified school-route stops more than 7 minutes after the scheduled time, grouped by length of delay.

(iv) Arrival before the bell: where it can be measured reliably, the percentage of trips serving a school that reached the school’s stop before the first bell, together with the number of trips observed and any known gaps in coverage.

(3) The Administration and the school system shall jointly establish and publish performance targets for the measures in paragraph (2). If a target is missed, the next report shall identify the affected routes or schools, the corrective action, the entity responsible for carrying it out, and the date progress will be reviewed.

(4) Any student outcome data published under this subsection shall be aggregated and subject to safeguards that prevent identification of individual students.

In this proposal, (2)(i) to (2)(iii) would be Agency benchmark measures (the tracker already estimates versions of them from the public feed); (2)(iv) is a Proposed student outcome that no agency reports today and the tracker cannot measure. The fiscal 2024 budget-bill report on supplemental trips (Question 3) shows MTA can already produce (iii) for some trips; the draft would make it recurring and add cancellations.

The tracker can show where buses were late or canceled. This proposal would require MTA and City Schools to show whether the service ran as planned and whether students reached class before the bell.
