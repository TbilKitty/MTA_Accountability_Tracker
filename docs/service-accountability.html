Baltimore, Maryland · Independent public-interest research

Every missed connection matters.

What counts as a late trip, a missed trip, or an unverified report—and why reporting barriers deserve attention.

Evidence standard: A missing GPS observation does not prove a bus never arrived. Rider reports, feed observations, and independently corroborated incidents are distinct categories.

The reporting gap: independent documentation of transit failures

Formal complaint counts measure reports received, not necessarily all service failures. Students and other riders may lack access, time, knowledge, or practical means to report incidents. The extent of underreporting has not yet been quantified and must not be described as a proven majority.

Maryland Public Information Act (MPIA) requests can obtain existing responsive records, subject to exemptions and retention practices. They cannot compel production of records that were never created; other agency operational data may nonetheless document a failure even without a complaint.

Research design

  1. Compare scheduled service against archived real-time trip and vehicle data, preserving data gaps.
  2. Collect voluntary, privacy-protective rider reports with incident time, route, approximate stop area, and optional corroboration.
  3. Separately request agency complaints, operational logs, dispatch records, service alerts, and relevant retention policies.
  4. Deduplicate events and label each verified, corroborated, unverified, or inconclusive.
  5. Report complaint-to-observed-incident ratios only for comparable geographic and temporal coverage; do not treat unreported events as automatically verified.
  6. Publish aggregate findings and methodology; keep identifying details of minors and private rider records restricted.

Legal caution

A failure to appear in a complaint dataset does not prove that an incident occurred or that an agency breached a legal duty. Evidence of an incident is distinct from proof of causation, damages, or legal liability.

Specific structural contention: the vehicle number

A no-show passenger cannot identify the fleet number of a bus never seen. Determine whether MTA's form requires or merely requests that number before making a categorical claim. See COMPLAINT_FORM_AUDIT.md and STRUCTURAL_REPORTING_BARRIERS.md.

Structural barriers to documenting missed bus service

Core contention

Complaint totals are counts of reports received, not a census of service failures. Students, particularly minors, may lack the time, administrative knowledge, internet or telephone access, or incentive to navigate an agency complaint process. A minor should not have to file a complaint for an operator to identify a scheduled trip that failed to provide service. The prevalence of these barriers is a research question, not a measured majority.

The unobservable bus number problem

A rider can report a route, direction, stop, scheduled arrival time and what occurred. When a vehicle never appears, the rider cannot observe its vehicle/fleet number. If a complaint form requests this number, the project must determine whether the field is required, optional, or simply confusing; preserve dated screenshots, instructions, and test results without submitting false complaints. A required vehicle-number field for no-show complaints would be a more serious accessibility/design defect than an optional field.

Records may exist even without complaints

An MPIA response containing no complaint for a trip does not establish that the trip ran, nor that no other records exist. Seek GTFS schedule versions, dispatch and operator assignment records, vehicle location and trip update histories, cancellation logs, customer-service classifications, complaint form instructions, record retention schedules, and known data gaps. Request relevant existing records; an MPIA request ordinarily does not require the agency to create new records.

Questions the project will test

  1. Can a rider report a missed trip without knowing a vehicle number?
  2. Are no-show and pass-by complaints accepted and separately coded?
  3. What fraction of independently corroborated incidents have a matching agency complaint, for periods where complaint data are available?
  4. What are the reporting barriers for school-age riders, measured through voluntary anonymous surveys rather than assumption?
  5. Can agency operations data identify a trip using route, stop, date, and scheduled time?

Legal and statistical limitations

Do not infer a constitutional or statutory violation solely from an incident. Identify the responsible transit and education entities and specific duties separately. A discrepancy between complaints and independently observed incidents is evidence of a reporting gap only after record coverage, retention, and matching rules have been checked. Preserve raw data, hashes, code versions, corrections and uncertainty. Do not expose a child's precise travel routine or identifiable details.

MTA complaint-form audit protocol

Status: not yet verified. Do not publicly assert that a vehicle number is mandatory until tested.

  • Record audit date, exact official form URL, browser/device, screenshot filename and SHA-256 digest.
  • Note whether vehicle number is present, mandatory, optional, conditionally required, or omitted.
  • Test no-show reporting path without submitting a fabricated complaint; document any validation errors.
  • Record available fields for route, direction, stop ID, date, scheduled time, description, and supporting evidence.
  • Determine whether minors can file, whether anonymous reports are possible, and whether a confirmation number is provided.
  • Preserve accessibility observations and language availability; do not infer violations from an informal test.
  • Recheck after any form change and retain dated historical versions.

The project rider form must never require a vehicle number for a no-show or pass-by report. The vehicle number may be optional for incidents where the rider observed it.
