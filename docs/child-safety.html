Getting Baltimore Students to School

A dependable ride is part of a dependable education. When a student relies on public transit, getting to class begins long before the first bell.

The school day starts at the bus stop

Every school day begins with a journey

Imagine a Baltimore student leaving home with a backpack, a class schedule, and enough time—on paper—to reach school before the first bell. The route is familiar. The stop is familiar. What the student cannot control is whether the scheduled bus arrives when expected.

This is an illustrative narrative, not a documented account of a particular student or incident. It shows why bus reliability matters to educational access.

Chapter 01 · Before the first bell

Getting to the bus stop

The student plans around a published schedule. A dependable bus allows enough time to travel, reach the school building, and prepare for class. When service is unpredictable, students may have to leave home earlier or build extra waiting time into their morning.

Chapter 02 · When the bus is late

Waiting instead of learning

A late bus can turn a carefully planned commute into a late arrival. If a student misses classroom instruction, the loss may include the opening lesson, an assignment, or a scheduled assessment. Waiting time and missed instructional time are different measures; the project tracks them separately.

Chapter 03 · When a connection is missed

One delay can affect the whole trip

For students whose journeys require a transfer, an initial delay may cause a missed connection and a much longer commute. A reliable transportation system should be evaluated from the student's perspective: can they reach school consistently, not merely whether a vehicle operated somewhere along the route?

Chapter 04 · The household ripple effect

Families fill the transportation gap

When a bus does not arrive as expected, a parent or caregiver may have to rearrange work, provide a ride, pay for alternative transportation, or coordinate another solution. These impacts should be measured carefully rather than assumed for every incident.

Chapter 05 · A better way to measure service

Make the school commute visible in the data

Accountability requires looking at service during school travel hours, particularly missed scheduled trips, significant delays, missed connections, and recurring problems along student-used routes. A rider complaint can help document a problem, but complaint totals alone cannot establish the full extent of unreliable service.

What matters for education

Three outcomes worth measuring

Attendance

Whether transportation problems contribute to late arrivals or missed school days.

Instructional time

Minutes of class actually missed, rather than assuming every minute spent waiting is lost classroom time.

Reliability by route

Whether scheduled school-hour trips operate consistently enough for students to plan around them.

Evidence before conclusions

How this project will document the problem

Our tracker combines published schedules, available real-time transit observations, service alerts, and carefully classified rider reports. A missing GPS observation is not automatically a missed bus. An unverified report is not presented as a confirmed failure. We will distinguish the date, route, stop, scheduled time, observed evidence, and confidence level for each finding.

Where student travel patterns are studied, analysis should use aggregated or anonymized information rather than identifying individual children or publishing their routines.

Research limitation: This page describes plausible educational effects and proposed measurements. It does not claim that a particular number of Baltimore students missed class because of MTA service without supporting records.

From the bus stop to the classroom

Reliable transportation is not separate from educational opportunity. For a student who depends on a bus, it is one of the conditions that makes regular attendance possible.
